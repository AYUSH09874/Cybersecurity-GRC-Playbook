# Internal Audit for Security GRC

## 1. Quick concept

An internal audit is a planned, documented evaluation against defined criteria. In GRC, it helps determine whether relevant policies, processes, and controls are designed and operating as intended. Audit should be evidence-based and objective; it is not informal advice or control-owner self-certification.

See [ISO 19011:2026](https://www.iso.org/standard/19011), the current published ISO guidance for management-system auditing as of this guide's review. Apply the version and professional requirements relevant to the actual audit program.

## 2. Lifecycle

1. Define audit universe, risk, objectives, scope, criteria, and independence safeguards.
2. Prepare the audit plan and test program.
3. Hold opening discussions and identify control owners.
4. Conduct walkthroughs and request evidence.
5. Test design and operation using appropriate sampling and corroboration.
6. Validate factual accuracy with owners while preserving auditor independence.
7. Report findings, evidence, risk, and action expectations.
8. Follow up and retest corrective actions.

## 3. Example control test

For quarterly privileged-access reviews, inspect the control description, identify the population source, choose a sample using the approved method, inspect reviewer evidence and decisions, trace selected removals to completed changes, and record the conclusion. If the population is unreliable or the sample indicates a broader issue, expand procedures or document the limitation.

## 4. Artifacts

Audit universe, risk-based audit plan, objective/scope/criteria, request list, walkthrough notes, test workpapers, sample-selection basis, evidence index, finding log, report, management response, corrective-action plan, and retest record.

## 5. Common pitfalls

- Testing only a sample selected by management without considering bias.
- Using an interview as the only evidence of an operating control.
- Omitting the scope, period, population, sample, or test steps.
- Writing a finding that only recommends a solution (“buy tool X”) without criteria, condition, and risk.
- Closing a finding without verifying effectiveness.

## 6. Interview questions

**Q: Difference between design and operating effectiveness?** Design asks whether the control, if performed as described, can address the risk. Operating effectiveness asks whether it actually operated as intended during the relevant period. Evidence differs for each.

**Q: Management disagrees with a finding. What do you do?** Validate facts and criteria, review additional evidence, correct factual errors, and document unresolved disagreement and management response. Do not suppress a supported finding simply because the owner dislikes it.
